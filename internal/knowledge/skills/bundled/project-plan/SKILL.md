# Project plan

A plan is useful when someone can look at it and know what to do next. Keep it that small.

## Define done

1. Write the goal as an outcome: "The shop takes online orders by 1 September."
2. Ask how the user will know it is finished. That is the acceptance test.
3. Note the fixed things: the date, the budget, the people available.
4. Write down what is out of scope. This saves more time than anything else.

## Break it down

- Split the goal into three to seven stages. Each ends in something you can see: a draft, a decision, a working part.
- Split each stage into tasks someone can finish in a day or two. A task starts with a verb and has one owner.
- Mark what depends on what. Find the chain of tasks that sets the end date.
- Find the riskiest or least-known part and put a small task early to test it.
- Add the tasks people forget: getting approval, waiting for a supplier, testing, telling the people affected.

## Estimate

Give each task a rough size. Add them along the longest chain and compare with the date. If it does not fit, say so now, and offer the choices: less scope, more help, or a later date. Do not shrink the estimates to make the plan fit.

Leave slack. A plan with none is already late.

## Write it down

- One line for the goal and the date.
- The stages, each with its tasks, owner and target date.
- The next three tasks, called out at the top.
- Risks: the two or three things most likely to go wrong, and what you would do.
- Open questions, and who will answer them.

Create the tasks on the board if you have one and the user agrees. Otherwise keep the plan as a file in the workspace.

## Keep it alive

Offer a short check each week: what moved, what is blocked, whether the date still holds. Update the plan when reality changes. A plan nobody updates is worse than none.
