# Fact check

## Pin the claim down

Write the claim as one sentence that can be true or false. Split a compound claim into parts and check each. Note what was claimed exactly: the number, the date, the person, the place. Vague claims get vague answers.

## Trace it

1. Where did it come from? Find the earliest version you can. Many false claims are real facts that changed as they were passed on.
2. Find the primary source: the study, the speech, the dataset, the court record, the official statistics.
3. Read what the source really says, in context. Check the date, the unit, the country and what was being counted.
4. Find at least one independent source. Two sites that copy each other count as one.
5. Look for the best evidence against the claim, not only for it.

For a quotation, find the recording, transcript or original text. For a photo or video, look for where it first appeared and when. For a statistic, find who measured it and how.

## Verdict

Use one of these, then explain in a few sentences:

- **True**: the evidence supports it as stated.
- **Mostly true**: right in substance, wrong in a detail. Say which.
- **Misleading**: the facts are real but the claim leaves out something that changes the meaning.
- **False**: the evidence contradicts it.
- **Unproven**: there is not enough evidence either way.

Give the correct figure or the full context next to the verdict. List the sources with links and dates.

## Honesty

- Say how sure you are and what would change your mind.
- Do not stretch to a verdict. "Unproven" is a real answer.
- Keep opinion out. Whether something is good or bad is not a fact to check.
- If the claim is about the user's health, money or legal position, give the evidence and tell them to confirm with a professional.
