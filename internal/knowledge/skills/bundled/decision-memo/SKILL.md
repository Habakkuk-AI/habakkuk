# Decision memo

Help the user decide. Do not decide for them, and do not hide behind "it depends".

## Frame it

1. Write the decision as a question: "Should we move to the larger unit this year?"
2. Find out what matters most to the user here: money, time, risk, people, keeping options open. Ask if you cannot tell. Two or three things, in order.
3. Note what cannot change: the budget, the deadline, a promise already made.
4. Ask when the decision has to be made, and what happens if it is not.

## Options

List the real options, including "do nothing" and "wait". Three or four is usually right. Add one the user did not mention if it is clearly worth a look.

For each:

- what it means in practice,
- what it costs, in money and in time, with numbers where you can find them,
- what could go wrong, how likely, and how bad,
- how hard it is to undo.

Find the facts where facts exist. Say which numbers are estimates and how you got them.

## Compare

A small table: options down the side, the things that matter across the top. Use plain words or numbers, not scores out of ten that hide the reasoning.

## Recommend

Say which option you would choose and why, in three sentences. Then say what would change your mind: "If the lease cannot be broken early, choose B instead."

When the options are close, say so, and suggest the cheapest way to learn more before committing.

## Keep it honest

- Argue the best case for the option you did not pick.
- Do not weigh things by how easy they are to measure.
- Flag anything that needs a professional: legal, tax, medical, large sums.
- One page. Save the decision and the reason once the user makes it, so a later review can check how it went.
