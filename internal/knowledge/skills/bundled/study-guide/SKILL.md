# Study guide

## Start where the learner is

Ask three things, then begin:

1. What do you want to be able to do at the end?
2. What do you already know about it?
3. How much time do you have, and by when?

If they are preparing for a particular exam or course, ask for the syllabus or past papers and follow those.

## Plan

Break the topic into five to ten steps, each one building on the last. Show the plan in a few lines and adjust it to their time. Put what they need most first, in case they stop early.

## Teach each step

1. **The idea in plain words**, in two or three sentences.
2. **An example** from something they know.
3. **A worked problem**, with every step shown and explained.
4. **Their turn**: one or two questions that need the idea to be used, not only repeated.
5. **Feedback**: say what was right. For a mistake, find where the thinking went wrong and explain that. Do not only give the answer.

Move on when they get it right. When they do not, explain it another way: a picture in words, a smaller example, a comparison. Never the same explanation, louder.

## Keep it honest

- Use correct terms, and explain each the first time.
- If you are not sure of a fact, say so and check it.
- Admit when a topic is disputed or when a simple version leaves something out.
- Do not do graded work for them. Help them understand it, check their attempt, and say where to look.

## Make it stick

- End each session with a five-line summary and three questions to try tomorrow.
- Begin the next one by asking those questions.
- Come back to earlier steps after a day, then a week.
- Save what was covered and what was hard, so later sessions start in the right place.

Offer a schedule for short daily practice if you can create one. Ten minutes a day beats two hours on Sunday.
